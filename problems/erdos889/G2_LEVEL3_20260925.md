## 0. Verdict (short)

* **Level 3: CLEAR.** No source I read or found proves, for all large n, that some term of the
  block has three distinct prime factors greater than its index k. That holds in every k-range: C
  log n, (log n)^A, or unrestricted (= "v₀(n) ≥ 3 eventually"). It also holds at every l, fixed or
  uniform.
  * The strongest published statements are all at level 2:
    * ErSe67: v₀(n) ≥ 2 for n ≥ 17, using only k ≤ 2.
    * Level 2 at l ≥ 1 by Langevin 1981 plus Lemma R (pass A), which is our Theorem A.
  * Every source after 1967 that mentions the problem treats v₀ → ∞ as open, and none records any
    progress past v₀ ≥ 2. Those sources are Guy 2004 B27, erdosproblems (edited 02 Jan 2026), and
    FC `erdos_889` / `general` (research open).
* **General level M ≥ 3: CLEAR.** For every M this is the open main problem, and at every l it is
  the open `general` variant.
* **Not OVERLAP, but there are adjacent statements the campaign must cite** (§3): the capital-V
  level-3 question (largest n with V₀(n) = 2), ErSe67's averaged function f(n), whole-block ω
  bounds, and #890.
* **Residual risk.** Several sources were not obtained (§6), notably [Er98] p. 178, Erdős 1976
  (Publ. Math. Debrecen), Erdős–Graham 1980 and the Shorey–Tijdeman 1986 book. Indirect evidence:
  erdosproblems cites [Er98] p. 178 and Guy, and still says only v₀ ≥ 2 is known.

## 1. Targets (exact)

v(n,k) = #{p prime : p | n+k, p > k} (FC `Erdos889.v`), and v_l(n) = sup_{k ≥ l} v(n,k).

* **L_M(K), level M, range K(n):** there is N0 such that every n ≥ N0 has some k with
  0 ≤ k ≤ K(n) and v(n,k) ≥ M. The l-version L_M^l(K) requires l ≤ k ≤ K(n); it can be fixed l
  or uniform in l.
  * The target is M = 3 with K = C log n, (log n)^A, or K unrestricted.
  * Since v(n,k) ≥ 3 forces n + k ≥ (k+1)(k+2)(k+3), we get k < n^{1/3} + 1. So the unrestricted
    (or "k = o(n)") version is exactly "v₀(n) ≥ 3 for all large n" (resp. v_l(n) ≥ 3).
  * L_M for every M at l = 0 is `erdos_889`; for every l it is `erdos_889.variants.general`.

**Links to other statements** (elementary; checked here by hand):

1. *Luccioli's inequality* (erdosproblems forum, 3 Jan 2026): v(n+1,k) ≤ v(n,k+1) + 1, hence
   v_{l+1}(n) ≥ v_l(n+1) − 1.
   * So L_M at level l implies L_{M−1} at level l+1, with the k-range shifted by 1.
   * In particular L_3 at l = 0 gives v₁(n) ≥ 2 eventually, which is our Theorem A qualitatively.
2. *V ≥ v.* A prime p > k has p^α > k. So L_3 at l = 0 implies that {n : V₀(n) = 2} is finite.
   ErSe67: "The largest n such that V₀(n) = 2 is probably very large." Guy B27: "What is the
   largest n such that V₀(n) = 2?"
3. *ErSe67's averaged function.* f(n) = max_k (1/(k+1)) Σ_{i≤k} v(n;i) ≤ v₀(n). So "f(n) > 2 for
   all large n" implies L_3 at l = 0. ErSe67 conjecture f(n) → ∞ "but this will be probably very
   difficult to prove".

### 2.3 The Sylvester–Schur family

The task asked whether any of these gives three distinct primes > k dividing the same term. The
family is Sylvester 1892, Schur, Faulkner 1966, Hanson 1973 and Laishram–Shorey 2004–06, and its
statements concern either:

* P(Δ), the largest prime factor: > k, > 1.5k (Hanson), > 1.95k (Laishram–Shorey); or
* ω(Δ), the number of distinct prime factors of the whole product.

None that I saw, in the surveys (rows 4–6) or in the papers read, assigns two or more primes > k
to the same term. Faulkner's and Hanson's original papers were not read (§6).

### 2.4 The only same-term multiplicity statement found

ErSe67 quotes Schinzel (Elem. Math. 14 (1959), Problem 31), via Pólya: "with the possible exception
of a finite number of cases amongst p₁⋯p_{k−1}p_{k+1} consecutive integers there is always one which
has more than k prime factors." It gives nothing for v, for three reasons:

* It counts all prime factors.
* The block length is e^{(1+o(1))k log k}, far larger than the primes involved, so these primes
  need not exceed the term's position.
* It is ineffective, because it rests on Pólya.

## 5. Method landscape

**Tools the literature uses for statements of this type.** None reaches more than one large prime
per term for all n.

1. **Combinatorial counting (Sylvester–Erdős).** Give each prime p ≤ k the term where it appears to
   the highest power; the product of the other k-smooth parts divides k!. This gives Sylvester and
   the ω(Δ) ≥ π(k) + c·π(k) bounds for n > k (Saradha–Shorey, Laishram–Shorey, with explicit π(x)
   estimates).
2. **Pólya / Thue–Siegel–Mahler (ineffective).** Gaps between S-units tend to ∞, so for fixed k
   and large n at most one term is k-smooth. This gives ω(Δ) ≥ k + π(k) − 1, and Schinzel's
   same-term statement.
3. **Baker's linear forms in logarithms (effective).** Used by Tijdeman 1973, Ramachandra
   1969–73, RST 1975/76 (with Hall's theorem for Grimm), Shorey 1974, Langevin 1975–81 and
   Shorey–Tijdeman 1989–92.
   * The method needs terms that are S-units for an explicit S, i.e. k-smooth terms.
   * Its outputs are ≤ π(k) or o(π(k)) k-smooth terms, Grimm-type systems of distinct
     representatives, P(Δ) ≫ k loglog n, and ω(Δ) ≥ k + (1−ε)π(k).
4. **Sieve and exponential sums.** Jutila and Shorey 1974 for the gap function f(k).
   Tao–Teräväinen 2025 and Lau 2026 use probabilistic sieve constructions of infinitely many n
   with few prime factors; that is the opposite direction to ours.
5. **abc-conditional.** Langevin and Granville (as cited in Shorey–Tijdeman 2016 §8), and that §8 itself: radical and
   largest-prime bounds for blocks.
6. **Hypothesis-H / prime-tuples constructions.** BSST 2009, Tao on #890. They produce extremal
   examples showing that fixed-k statements are tight.

**Explicit barriers stated in the literature:**

* ErSe67 on v₀ → ∞: "we are very far from being able to prove this". On v₁ = 1 and 330: "we have
  no method of proving this".
* ErSe71 Thm 1: even weak Grimm would imply p_{i+1} − p_i < c(p_i/log p_i)^{1/2}, so there is "not
  much hope to prove Grimm's conjecture in the 'near future'".
* Surveys (row 6): for k = 2, infinitely many Mersenne primes would give ω(Δ₁) = 2 infinitely
  often. So fixed-k lower bounds past Sylvester are blocked.
* Tao (#890 forum): the fixed-k liminf question is a variant of the prime tuples conjecture.

**Our structural observations.** These are not in the literature and have had a same-vendor check
only. They bear directly on the OPERATIONS campaign plan ("extend Lemma R to two large primes per
term + multi-log Matveev; fallback RST block-product counting").

* **(a) The pigeonhole / block-counting route.** Primes > K divide at most one of n, …, n+K, so
  Σ_{i=0}^{K} v(n,i) ≥ ω_{>K}(n(n+1)⋯(n+K)).
  * L_3 would follow from ω_{>K} ≥ 2(K+1) + 1, which is also ErSe67's f(n) > 2.
  * Every known lower bound gives ω_{>K} ≈ K, about one large prime per term (§2.1 rows 3–5).
  * For fixed K, #890 and ErSe67 (2) conjecture that ≈ K is attained infinitely often.
  * So the fallback needs a factor-2 improvement over all known block counts in the log range. I
    found no result of that shape.
* **(b) The Lemma R route under (H3): v(n,k) ≤ 2 for 1 ≤ k ≤ K.**
  * For each prime p ≤ K with p ∤ n, the least k₀(p) with p | n + k₀ satisfies k₀ < p. The term
    n + k₀ carries p and at most one other prime q > k₀.
  * The map p ↦ k₀(p) is at most 2-to-1, and a fibre of size 2 forces the term to be K-smooth.
  * Hence at least (π(K) − ω(n))/2 terms have the form (K-smooth)·(1 or q^b with q > K), i.e. their
    second-largest distinct prime factor is ≤ K.
  * A contradiction needs an upper bound o(π(K)) for such terms, for all large n. Baker, Matveev
    and Langevin see only the K-smooth ones. The unknown prime q is not in any fixed S, so a
    multi-log Matveev estimate does not apply to these terms as they stand.
  * No source bounds the number of terms with at most one prime factor > k in a block. **This is
    the gap the campaign must close.** It will need a new idea, not a longer linear form.
* **(c) The sieve / short-interval route.**
  * L_M at l = 0 would follow from: every interval (x, x + x^θ] with Mθ < 1 contains an integer
    with ≥ M distinct prime factors > x^θ. For all M, that would give the main conjecture.
  * I found no such theorem. The needed primes exceed the interval length, and Type-I sieve
    information is available only for moduli below the interval length. So this seems beyond
    current methods (our assessment).
* **(d) Conditional obstruction for l ≥ 1.**
  * Fix K0. Let M = lcm(1..K0)·∏_{p≤K0} p and n = Mt, so that n + i = i·((M/i)t + 1).
  * The linear forms (M/i)t + 1, 1 ≤ i ≤ K0, are admissible. Every p ≤ K0 divides M/i, so each form
    is ≡ 1 (mod p). For p > K0 the forms have at most K0 < p roots mod p.
  * So Dickson's conjecture gives infinitely many n with v(n,i) = 1 for all 1 ≤ i ≤ K0.
  * Hence any theorem at level ≥ 2 with l ≥ 1 needs a k-range K(n) → ∞; bounded ranges are
    conditionally false. The C log n range of the target is compatible with this.
