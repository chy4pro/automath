## 0. Statement, conventions, and an erratum

$\sigma$ is the sum of divisors, $\sigma^*$ the sum of *unitary* divisors
($d\mid n$ with $\gcd(d,n/d)=1$); both are multiplicative with
$\sigma(p^e)=1+p+\dots+p^e$ and $\sigma^*(p^e)=1+p^e$. Put

$$A=\{n\ge 1:\ \sigma(n)=2\sigma^*(n)\}.$$

OEIS A063880 conjectures

* **(a)** every $n\in A$ satisfies $n\equiv 108 \pmod{216}$;
* **(b)** $108$ is the only *primitive* member of $A$ (no proper divisor in $A$).

**Erratum.** The term list quoted in the local task file
(`prompts/solve_task_63880.md`, "108, 540, 756, 972, 1188, 1620, …") contains two
non-members: $\sigma(972)-2\sigma^*(972)=108$ and
$\sigma(1620)-2\sigma^*(1620)=162$. The true initial segment is
$108\cdot m$ with $m$ squarefree and $\gcd(m,6)=1$:
$108, 540, 756, 1188, 1404, 1836, 2052, 2484,\dots$ (v1 already flagged both).
Both non-members are nevertheless $\equiv 108\pmod{216}$, so no conjecture is harmed.

Throughout write, for a prime power $p^e\parallel n$,

$$\rho(p,e)\;=\;\frac{\sigma(p^e)}{p^e+1}\;=\;\frac{p^{e+1}-1}{(p-1)(p^e+1)},
\qquad P(p)\;=\;\frac{p}{p-1}.$$

By multiplicativity,

$$n\in A\iff \prod_{p^e\parallel n}\rho(p,e)=2. \tag{1}$$

Three facts are used constantly and are immediate from the definition:

$$\rho(p,1)=1,\qquad \rho(p,2)<\rho(p,3)<\dots<P(p),\qquad
P(p)-\rho(p,e)=\frac{p+1}{(p-1)(p^e+1)}=:\delta(p,e)>0. \tag{2}$$

So $\rho(p,\cdot)$ is strictly increasing with supremum $P(p)$, never attained.

## 1. Reduction to powerful numbers

Let $K(n)=\prod_{p^e\parallel n,\ e\ge 2}p^e$ be the **powerful core** of $n$.

**Lemma 1.** $n\in A\iff K(n)\in A$. In particular every primitive member of $A$
is powerful, and $n\mapsto K(n)$ maps $A$ onto the set of powerful members of $A$.

*Proof.* Exponent-one primes contribute the factor $\rho(p,1)=1$ to (1). $\square$

**Reformulation.** Conjectures (a) and (b) are statements about the powerful
members of $A$ only. §3 shows both are *equivalent* to

> **(c)** $108$ is the only powerful member of $A$.

Everything below therefore concerns powerful $n$: all exponents $e_i\ge 2$.

## 2. Theorem A — the case $\omega\le 2$, unconditionally

> **Theorem A.** The only powerful $n\in A$ with at most two distinct prime
> factors is $n=108=2^2\cdot 3^3$.

*Proof.* Since $\rho(p,e)<P(p)$, (1) forces $\prod_{p\mid n}P(p)>2$.

*One prime.* $P(p)\le 2$ for every prime, and $\rho(p,e)<P(p)$, so the product
cannot be $2$.

*Two primes $p<q$.* If both were odd then
$\prod P\le P(3)P(5)=\tfrac32\cdot\tfrac54=\tfrac{15}{8}<2$. Hence $p=2$; write
$n=2^aq^f$ with $a,f\ge2$ and $\alpha:=2^a$. From (1),
$\rho(q,f)=2/\rho(2,a)=\dfrac{2(\alpha+1)}{2\alpha-1}$, i.e.

$$\rho(q,f)-1=\frac{3}{2\alpha-1}.$$

On the other hand $\rho(q,f)-1=\dfrac{q\,(q^{f-1}-1)}{(q-1)(q^f+1)}$. Cross-multiplying,

$$q\,(q^{f-1}-1)\,(2\alpha-1)\;=\;3\,(q-1)\,(q^f+1). \tag{3}$$

Reduce (3) modulo $q$: the left side is $\equiv 0$, the right side is
$\equiv 3\cdot(-1)\cdot 1=-3$. Hence $q\mid 3$, i.e. **$q=3$**.

With $q=3$, (3) becomes $(3^{f-1}-1)(2\alpha-1)=2(3^f+1)$, so with $Z=3^{f-1}$

$$2\alpha-1=\frac{6Z+2}{Z-1}=6+\frac{8}{Z-1},$$

and $Z-1=3^{f-1}-1$ must divide $8$. For $f\ge2$ this gives $3^{f-1}\in\{3,9\}$,
i.e. $f\in\{2,3\}$. For $f=2$: $2\alpha-1=10$, impossible ($2\alpha-1$ is odd). For $f=3$:
$2\alpha-1=7$, $\alpha=4$, $a=2$. Thus $n=2^2\cdot3^3=108$, and conversely
$\rho(2,2)\rho(3,3)=\tfrac75\cdot\tfrac{10}{7}=2$. $\square$

Theorem A strictly contains v1's Theorem 2 (which handled only the case where the
part of $n$ coprime to $6$ is squarefree, i.e. the core supported on $\{2,3\}$);
Theorem A allows *any* two primes and *any* exponents.

**Remark (why the mod-$q$ step is the engine).** For a general solution with
primes $p_1<\dots<p_k$, clearing denominators in (1) gives
$\prod_i(p_i^{e_i+1}-1)=2\prod_i(p_i-1)(p_i^{e_i}+1)$; reducing modulo $p_j$ shows

$$p_j\ \Big|\ \prod_{i\ne j}(p_i^{e_i+1}-1)\;-\;2\prod_{i\ne j}(p_i-1)(p_i^{e_i}+1)
\qquad(1\le j\le k). \tag{4}$$

Equivalently: if $\rho(p_j,e_j)=u/v$ in lowest terms then $p_j\mid u-v$. In
Theorem A the relevant $u-v$ collapsed to the constant $3$; in general it does not.

## 4. Lemma C — the $\{2,3\}$-part is pinned down

### 4.1 Statement

> **Lemma C.** Let $n$ be a powerful member of $A$ with $6\mid n$, and write
> $2^a\parallel n$, $3^b\parallel n$ (so $a,b\ge2$). Then $a=2$ and $b\in\{2,3\}$.
> Moreover
> * $b=3$ $\iff$ $n=108$;
> * $b=2$ $\iff$ $n=36M$ with $M>1$ powerful, $\gcd(M,6)=1$, and
>   $$\prod_{p^e\parallel M}\rho(p,e)=\frac{100}{91}. \tag{5}$$

*Proof.* Put $\alpha=2^a$, $\beta=3^b$. Since all other factors in (1) are $>1$,

$$\rho(2,a)\rho(3,b)=\frac{2\alpha-1}{\alpha+1}\cdot\frac{3\beta-1}{2(\beta+1)}\;\le\;2 ,$$

with equality iff $n=2^a3^b$. Clearing denominators, this is equivalent to

$$T(\alpha,\beta):=2\alpha\beta-6\alpha-7\beta-3\le 0 .$$

Write $T=\alpha(2\beta-6)-(7\beta+3)$. For $b\ge2$ we have $\beta\ge9$, hence $2\beta-6>0$ and $T$
is increasing in $\alpha$; since $\alpha\ge4$,

$$T\;\ge\;4(2\beta-6)-(7\beta+3)\;=\;\beta-27 .$$

Therefore $T\le0$ forces $\beta\le27$, i.e. $b\in\{2,3\}$:

* $b=3$ ($\beta=27$): $T=48(\alpha-4)$, so $T\le0$ forces $\alpha=4$ and then $T=0$, i.e.
  $\rho(2,2)\rho(3,3)=2$ exactly, so $n$ has no further prime factor: $n=108$.
* $b=2$ ($\beta=9$): $T=12\alpha-66$, so $T\le0$ forces $\alpha=4$, and $T=-18<0$, so $n>36$.
  Then the tail target is $2/(\rho(2,2)\rho(3,2))=2/\frac{91}{50}=\frac{100}{91}$.
* $b\ge4$: $T\ge \beta-27\ge 54>0$, impossible. $\square$

Note the identity behind the $b=3$ case: $T=0\iff (2\alpha-7)(2\beta-6)=48$, the
factorisation already used in v1.

**Corollary C1.** Every powerful $n\in A$ divisible by $6$ has $v_2(n)=2$. So the
"$4\parallel n$" half of conjecture (a) is *unconditional* whenever $6\mid n$.

### 4.2 Consequences for the tail (5)

**Corollary C2.** Every prime of $M$ in (5) is $\ge 11$.

*Proof.* Each factor of (5) is $\le 100/91$, and $\rho(p,e)\ge\rho(p,2)=\frac{p^2+p+1}{p^2+1}$.
Now $\frac{p^2+p+1}{p^2+1}\le\frac{100}{91}\iff 9p^2-91p+9\ge0\iff p\ge 10.01\ldots$ $\square$

**Corollary C3 ($\omega(M)=1$ is impossible).** If $M=p^e$ then
$\rho(p,e)=100/91$ in lowest terms, so by (4) $p\mid 100-91=9$, forcing $p=3$,
contradicting C2. Hence:

> **Theorem D.** If $6\mid n$, $n$ powerful, $n\in A$ and $\omega(n)\le 3$, then $n=108$.

**Corollary C4 ($\omega(M)=2$).** If $M=p^eq^f$ with $11\le p<q$ then
$P(p)^2>P(p)P(q)>100/91$ gives $\left(\frac{p}{p-1}\right)^2>\frac{100}{91}$,
i.e. $p\le 19$: $p\in\{11,13,17,19\}$. For each such $p$, $q$ satisfies
$P(q)>\frac{100/91}{P(p)}$, which bounds $q$ (e.g. $p=19\Rightarrow q\le23$,
$p=17\Rightarrow q\le29$, $p=13\Rightarrow q\le 70$; for $p=11$ one first notes
$\rho(11,3)=\frac{122}{111}>\frac{100}{91}$, so $e=2$ is forced, whence
$\rho(q,f)=\frac{12200}{12103}$ and (4) gives $q\mid 97$, i.e. $q=97$, which is
excluded because $\rho(97,f)\ge\rho(97,2)=\frac{9507}{9410}>\frac{12200}{12103}$).
The remaining finitely many pairs are killed by the exact solver of §6
(6 prime sets in total, no solutions). Hence:

> **Theorem E.** If $6\mid n$, $n$ powerful, $n\in A$ and $\omega(n)\le 4$, then $n=108$.

### 4.3 Proof of Theorem B

*(a) $\Rightarrow$ (c).* Let $n$ be a powerful member of $A$. By (a),
$n\equiv108\pmod{216}$, i.e. $n=108(2t+1)$, so $v_2(n)=2$ and $v_3(n)\ge3$. In
particular $6\mid n$, so Lemma C applies and gives $v_3(n)\in\{2,3\}$; combined
with $v_3(n)\ge3$ we get $v_3(n)=3$ and hence $n=108$.

*(c) $\Rightarrow$ (b).* If $108$ is the only powerful member of $A$, then by
Lemma 1 every $n\in A$ has $K(n)=108$, so $108\mid n$; since $108\in A$, a
primitive member must equal $108$.

*(b) $\Rightarrow$ (a).* Let $n\in A$. Some divisor of $n$ is a minimal element of
$A$ under divisibility, hence primitive, hence $=108$ by (b); so $108\mid n$.
The same argument applied to $K(n)\in A$ (Lemma 1) gives $108\mid K(n)$. Now $K(n)$ is powerful and
divisible by $6$, so Lemma C gives $v_2(K(n))=2$ and $v_3(K(n))\in\{2,3\}$; as
$27\mid108\mid K(n)$ we get $v_3(K(n))=3$. Since $v_2(n)=v_2(K(n))$ and
$v_3(n)=v_3(K(n))$ (both exponents are $\ge2$), $n=2^2 3^3 m$ with $\gcd(m,6)=1$,
i.e. $n=108m$ with $m$ odd, i.e. $n\equiv108\pmod{216}$. $\square$

So the folklore implication (a)$\Rightarrow$(b) is now an equivalence, and both
are equivalent to the single powerful-number statement (c).

## 5. Exact $2$-adic and $3$-adic constraints (v1, re-verified)

For odd $p$ put $D_2(e)=v_2(\sigma(p^e))-v_2(1+p^e)$; it does not depend on $p$:

$$D_2(e)=\begin{cases}-1,&e\text{ even},\\ v_2(e+1)-1,&e\text{ odd},\end{cases}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad \sum_{\substack{p^e\parallel n\\ p\ \mathrm{odd}}}D_2(e)=1. \tag{6}$$

For $p\ne3$ put $D_3(p,e)=v_3(\sigma(p^e))-v_3(1+p^e)$; then

$$D_3(p,e)=\begin{cases} v_3(e+1),&p\equiv1\ (3),\\ 0,&p\equiv-1\ (3),\ e\text{ even},\\
v_3(e+1)-v_3(e),&p\equiv-1\ (3),\ e\text{ odd},\end{cases}
\qquad \sum_{\substack{p^e\parallel n\\ p\ne3}}D_3(p,e)=0. \tag{7}$$

Both are re-verified in PART 5 of the script. Combining (6) with Lemma C:

**Corollary C5.** In the situation (5) ($n=36M$) one has
$\sum_{p^e\parallel M}D_2(e)=2$. Since $D_2(e)=-1$ for even $e$ and $D_2(e)=0$ for
$e\equiv1\pmod4$, at least one prime of $M$ carries an exponent $e\equiv3\pmod 4$;
and if exactly one prime of $M$ does, with $j$ primes of $M$ carrying even
exponents, then $v_2(e+1)=j+3$, so $e\ge 2^{j+3}-1\ge 7$. Together with C2 that
prime power is $\ge 11^7>1.9\cdot10^7$.

## 6. Two window lemmas, and a decision procedure

Fix a target $\tau>1$ and suppose $\prod_{i=1}^{k}\rho(p_i,e_i)=\tau$ with
distinct primes $p_i$ and $e_i\ge2$. Write $P_i=P(p_i)$, $\delta_i=\delta(p_i,e_i)$,
$\mathrm{rest}_i=\prod_{j\ne i}P_j$ and $W=\prod_i P_i-\tau$. Then $W>0$ and:

> **(L1)** $\displaystyle \delta_i\le \frac{W}{\mathrm{rest}_i}$ for **every** $i$.
> **(L2)** $\displaystyle \delta_i\ge \frac{W}{k\,\mathrm{rest}_i}$ for **at least one** $i$.

*Proof.* (L1): $\tau=(P_i-\delta_i)\prod_{j\ne i}(P_j-\delta_j)\le(P_i-\delta_i)\mathrm{rest}_i$,
so $\delta_i\le P_i-\tau/\mathrm{rest}_i=W/\mathrm{rest}_i$.
(L2): for $0<b_j\le a_j$ one has $\prod a_j-\prod b_j\le\sum_j (a_j-b_j)\prod_{l\ne j}a_l$;
with $a_j=P_j$, $b_j=P_j-\delta_j$ this reads $W\le\sum_j\delta_j\,\mathrm{rest}_j\le k\max_j \delta_j\,\mathrm{rest}_j$. $\square$

Since $\delta(p,e)=\frac{p+1}{(p-1)(p^e+1)}$ is strictly decreasing in $e$ with
limit $0$, (L1) is a *lower* bound and (L2) an *upper* bound on the exponent.
Consequently:

> **Decision procedure `solve_set`.** For a *given* finite prime set $S$ and a
> given $\tau$, all exponent vectors with $\prod\rho(p_i,e_i)=\tau$ can be found
> by: branch over which $i$ realises (L2); for that $i$ the exponent lies in the
> finite window given by (L1)+(L2); fix it, and recurse on $S\setminus\{p_i\}$
> with target $\tau/\rho(p_i,e_i)$. This terminates (the set shrinks) and is
> **complete and unconditional — no exponent bound is assumed.**

This is the key gain over a size-bounded search: once the prime *support* is
known, arbitrarily large exponents are handled.

For enumerating supports we use two necessary conditions, both exponent-free.
If the remaining $m$ primes are all $\ge p$ and their product of $\rho$'s lies in
$(t_{\mathrm{lo}},t_{\mathrm{up}}]$, then

$$\rho(p,2)\le t_{\mathrm{up}}\quad(\text{lower bound on }p),
\qquad \prod_{j=1}^{m}P(q_j)>t_{\mathrm{lo}}\quad(\text{upper bound on }p), \tag{8}$$

$q_1<\dots<q_m$ being the $m$ smallest primes $\ge p$; and after choosing $p$ one
may propagate $t_{\mathrm{up}}\mapsto t_{\mathrm{up}}/\rho(p,2)$ and
$t_{\mathrm{lo}}\mapsto t_{\mathrm{lo}}/s$, where $s$ is the supremum of the
admissible $\rho(p,e)$ (namely $P(p)$, or the largest $\rho(p,e)$ below
$t_{\mathrm{up}}$ when $P(p)>t_{\mathrm{up}}$).

The enumeration is finite **iff** $t_{\mathrm{lo}}$ stays $>1$; that is the exact
point where the method fails (see §8).

**The two engines actually run.**

* *Engine 1 (exponent-free).* Enumerate supports by (8) alone, then decide each
  support with `solve_set`. Whenever the enumeration reports no gap, the verdict
  is **unconditional in every exponent and in every prime**.
* *Engine 2 (exact residual + exponent-free tail).* Walk the primes in increasing
  order keeping the **exact** residual $t$; at each prime run the exponent loop
  $e=2,3,\dots$ while $\rho(p,e)\le t$. That loop terminates by itself exactly
  when $P(p)>t$. When it does not ($P(p)\le t$), stop it at some $E$ and treat
  the tail $e\ge E+1$ by engine 1: the residual after $p$ then lies in
  $\bigl(t/P(p),\,t/\rho(p,E+1)\bigr]$, enumerate the remaining supports from that
  interval and finish each complete support with `solve_set`. **Hence $E$ is only
  a bookkeeping split, not a hypothesis:** engine 2 is unconditional in the
  exponents too, and its only genuine restriction is the explicit bound on
  $v_2(n)$ (Obstacle 1) plus any support-enumeration gap it reports.

**The last prime of a support needs no sieve.** If exactly one prime $q$ is still
unknown and the residual is the *exact* rational $t=u/v>1$ (put $d=u-v\ge1$), then
the two necessary conditions $\rho(q,2)\le t$ and $P(q)>t$ read
$$d\,q^2-v\,q+d\ \ge\ 0,\qquad q\ <\ \frac{u}{d},$$
so $q$ lies in an explicit integer window whose length is
$1-\tfrac1d+\tfrac dv<2$: **at most two candidates, however astronomically large
they are.** Each is tested for primality, and then $\rho(q,g)=t$ is solved exactly
from $q^{g}+1=\dfrac{(q+1)/(q-1)}{P(q)-t}$. This is what lets the search reach
arbitrarily large primes at the final step.

## 8. Named obstacles

**Obstacle 1 — the $2$-part is not bounded by inequalities.**
Every method here bounds primes through the residual target
$t=\tau/\prod(\text{chosen }\rho)$, via $p<t/(t-1)$. If $2^a\parallel n$ then the
residual carried by the odd part is

$$\frac{2}{\rho(2,a)}=1+\frac{3}{2^{a+1}-1}\ \longrightarrow\ 1\quad(a\to\infty),$$

so as $a$ grows the odd primes admitted grow like $2^{a}$ and the search region
is unbounded. Concretely, for $n=2^ap^eq^f$ with $5\le p<q$ the inequalities do
pin the scale of $p$ — from $2<\rho(2,a)P(p)P(q)$ one gets $p<\tfrac43(2^a+1)$,
and from the positivity of the quantity in (4) for $j=q$ one gets
$2^a<\frac{p(3p-1)}{2(p-1)}$, so $p\asymp 2^a$ — but they do not *empty* the
window. **What would break it:** a bound on $a$, e.g. via
the fact that every prime factor $\ell>3$ of the Mersenne number $2^{a+1}-1$ must
divide $\prod_{p^e\parallel n,\,p\ \mathrm{odd}}(p^e+1)$ — a factor-chain/Zsygmondy
argument, not an inequality.

**Obstacle 2 — $\omega(n)$ is not bounded either.**
The two necessary conditions used in (8) read, to first order,
$\sum_{p\mid n}\frac1p\lesssim\log\tau$ and $\sum_{p\mid n}\frac1{p-1}\gtrsim\log\tau$.
These differ only by $\sum 1/p^2$, so they are simultaneously satisfiable by
arbitrarily large sets of large primes (e.g. all primes in $[P,P^2]$). Hence no
inequality of this shape can bound the number of prime factors, and any complete
proof must control the *exact* rational cancellation, not just the size.
The sharpest self-contained sub-problem is (5):

> Does there exist a powerful $M$ coprime to $6$ with
> $\displaystyle\prod_{p^e\parallel M}\rho(p,e)=\frac{100}{91}$ ?
> (All its prime factors are $\ge 11$; some exponent is $\equiv3\bmod 4$;
> and $M$ has at least three prime factors.)
> A negative answer *is* the conjecture restricted to $6\mid n$; a positive answer
> would disprove (a), (b) and (c) simultaneously.

**Obstacle 3 — the residual can hug $1$ from above.**
Even at fixed $\tau$ the support enumeration stalls as soon as the chosen prefix
can already exhaust the target ($t_{\mathrm{lo}}\le1$ in §6). For $\tau=100/91$
this first happens at $\omega(M)=3$; for $\tau=2$ it happens at the support
$\{3,5,7\}$ (since $\tfrac32\cdot\tfrac54\cdot\tfrac76=\tfrac{35}{16}>2$). Up to
$\omega=3$ the exact-residual engine still closes every branch, which is why (S2)
is unconditional; at $\omega=4$ this is exactly what leaves the $134$ open
branches of (S3).
