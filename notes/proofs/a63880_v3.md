## 2. Lemma V — the $\ell$-adic identity for every prime $\ell$

### 2.1 Statement

For primes $p\ne\ell$ and $e\ge1$ put $D_\ell(p,e)=v_\ell(\sigma(p^e))-v_\ell(p^e+1)$,
and $D_\ell(\ell,e)=0$.

> **Lemma V.**
> **(a)** $\ell=2$ (so $p$ odd, independent of $p$):
> $$D_2(p,e)=\begin{cases}-1,&e\text{ even},\\ v_2(e+1)-1,&e\text{ odd}.\end{cases}$$
> **(b)** $\ell$ odd, $p\ne\ell$. Let $d=\operatorname{ord}_\ell(p)$ and
> $c=v_\ell(p^d-1)\ge1$. Then
> $$D_\ell(p,e)=\begin{cases}
> v_\ell(e+1), & d=1,\\[2pt]
> 0, & d=2,\ e\text{ even},\\
> v_\ell(e+1)-v_\ell(e), & d=2,\ e\text{ odd},\\[2pt]
> c+v_\ell(e+1), & d\ge3,\ d\mid e+1,\\
> -\bigl(c+v_\ell(e)\bigr), & d\ge3,\ 2\mid d,\ e\equiv \tfrac d2\ (\mathrm{mod}\ d),\\
> 0, & d\ge3,\ \text{otherwise.}
> \end{cases}$$
> The last two non-zero cases for $d\ge3$ are mutually exclusive.

*Proof.* **(b)** Since $d\mid\ell-1$ we have $\ell\nmid d$, so lifting-the-exponent
gives, for $m\ge1$,
$$v_\ell(p^m-1)=\begin{cases}c+v_\ell(m),&d\mid m,\\ 0,&\text{else.}\end{cases}$$
Hence $v_\ell(\sigma(p^e))=v_\ell(p^{e+1}-1)-v_\ell(p-1)$, where $v_\ell(p-1)=c$ if
$d=1$ and $0$ otherwise. Also $v_\ell(p^e+1)=v_\ell(p^{2e}-1)-v_\ell(p^e-1)$, which is
$0$ if $d\mid e$ (both terms equal $c+v_\ell(e)$, using $v_\ell(2e)=v_\ell(e)$ for odd
$\ell$), equals $c+v_\ell(e)$ if $d\mid 2e$ but $d\nmid e$ (equivalently $d$ even and
$e\equiv d/2\bmod d$), and is $0$ if $d\nmid2e$. Assembling the cases gives the table.
Exclusivity: $d\mid e+1$ and $e\equiv d/2\ (d)$ force $d/2\equiv-1\ (d)$, i.e.
$d\mid \tfrac d2+1$, whence $d\le2$.

**(a)** $p$ odd. If $e$ is even then $p^e\equiv1\ (8)$, so $v_2(p^e+1)=1$, while
$\sigma(p^e)$ is a sum of $e+1$ (an odd number of) odd terms, hence odd: $D_2=-1$.
If $e$ is odd then $p^e+1=(p+1)(p^{e-1}-p^{e-2}+\dots+1)$ with the second factor a
sum of $e$ odd terms, hence odd, so $v_2(p^e+1)=v_2(p+1)$; and LTE for $\ell=2$
with $m=e+1$ even gives $v_2(p^{e+1}-1)=v_2(p-1)+v_2(p+1)+v_2(e+1)-1$, so
$v_2(\sigma(p^e))=v_2(p+1)+v_2(e+1)-1$ and $D_2=v_2(e+1)-1$. $\square$

Machine check: `48 734` cases, $\ell$ over $14$ primes, $p$ over the first $60$
primes, $e\le59$ — 0 mismatches (PART 1).

### 2.2 The sum rules

> **Corollary V1 (PROVED).** For $n\in A$ and every prime $\ell$,
> $\displaystyle\sum_{p^e\parallel n}D_\ell(p,e)=v_\ell(2)$.
> **Corollary V2 (PROVED).** For $M$ as in (5) and every prime $\ell$,
> $$\sum_{p^e\parallel M}D_\ell(p,e)=v_\ell(100)-v_\ell(91)=
> \begin{cases}+2,&\ell=2,\\ +2,&\ell=5,\\ -1,&\ell=7,\\ -1,&\ell=13,\\ 0,&\text{else.}\end{cases}$$

*Proof.* $n\in A$ says $\prod\sigma(p^e)=2\prod(p^e+1)$; take $v_\ell$. Likewise
$91\prod_{p^e\parallel M}\sigma(p^e)=100\prod_{p^e\parallel M}(p^e+1)$. $\square$

V1 with $\ell=2,3$ is exactly v2's (6),(7). The point of v3 is that **the same
machinery is available at $\ell=5,7,13$**, and at $\ell=5,7,13$ the right-hand side
is non-zero, so each of those primes forces structure.

### 2.3 The $2$-adic refinement

> **Corollary V3 (PROVED; refines v2's C5).** Write the exponents of $M$ as
> $e_1,\dots,e_k$ and let $j=\#\{i:e_i\text{ even}\}$. Then
> $$\sum_{e_i\ \mathrm{odd}}\bigl(v_2(e_i+1)-1\bigr)=j+2\ \ge\ 2 .$$
> Consequently **either at least two exponents of $M$ are $\equiv3\pmod4$, or at
> least one is $\equiv7\pmod8$** (so $\ge 7$).

*Proof.* By V2 at $\ell=2$, $-j+\sum_{e_i\text{ odd}}(v_2(e_i+1)-1)=2$. Every
summand is $\ge0$; it is $\ge1$ iff $e_i\equiv3\ (4)$ and $\ge2$ iff $e_i\equiv7\ (8)$.
A total of $\ge2$ therefore needs one summand $\ge2$ or two summands $\ge1$. $\square$

v2's C5 gave only "at least one exponent $\equiv3\bmod4$". Machine check: all
$1764$ exponent multisets from $\{2,\dots,25\}^{\le4}$ with $\sum D_2=2$ (PART 2).

## 3. What $\ell=7,5,13$ force

### 3.1 The $7$-adic dichotomy

> **Theorem G7 (PROVED).** Let $M$ be as in (5). Then $M$ has a prime power
> $p^e\parallel M$ with either
> * **(i)** $p\equiv3$ or $5\pmod 7$ and $e\equiv3\pmod 6$; or
> * **(ii)** $p\equiv6\pmod7$ and $e\equiv7\pmod{14}$ — so $e\ge7$ and, since
>   $p\ge11$ (C2), $p^e\ge11^7=19\,487\,171$.
>
> In case (i) the smallest admissible prime is $17$ (as $11\equiv4$, $13\equiv6$),
> so $p^e\ge17^3=4913$.

*Proof.* $\sum_{p^e\parallel M}D_7(p,e)=-1<0$, so some term is negative. For
$\ell=7$, $d=\operatorname{ord}_7(p)\in\{1,2,3,6\}$. By Lemma V, $d=1$ gives
$v_7(e+1)\ge0$ and $d=3$ gives $c+v_7(e+1)\ge0$ or $0$ — never negative (a negative
value needs $d$ even). For $d=2$ (i.e. $p\equiv6\bmod7$), $D_7=0$ for even $e$ and
$v_7(e+1)-v_7(e)$ for odd $e$, which is negative iff $7\mid e$; with $e$ odd this
is $e\equiv7\pmod{14}$. For $d=6$ (i.e. $p\equiv3,5\bmod7$), $D_7<0$ iff
$e\equiv d/2=3\pmod 6$. $\square$

Independently of Cor. V3 this says: **$M$ cannot have all exponents equal to $2$**,
and more sharply, $M$ has an exponent $\equiv3\ (6)$ or $\equiv7\ (14)$ attached to
a prime in an explicit residue class mod $7$.

### 3.2 The $5$-adic condition

> **Theorem G5 (PROVED).** $M$ has a prime power $p^e\parallel M$ with $D_5(p,e)>0$,
> hence with one of
> * $p\equiv2,3\pmod5$ and $e\equiv3\pmod4$;
> * $p\equiv1\pmod5$ and $e\equiv4\pmod5$;
> * $p\equiv4\pmod5$ and $e\equiv9\pmod{10}$.
>
> In every case $e\ge3$.

*Proof.* $\sum D_5=+2>0$ forces a positive term; read off Lemma V for
$d=\operatorname{ord}_5(p)\in\{1,2,4\}$. $\square$

Note the coherence with Cor. V3: the first (and cheapest) class is again
$e\equiv3\pmod4$.

### 3.3 $\ell=13$ gives nothing

> **Remark 13 (PROVED).** $\sum D_{13}=-1$ forces a negative $D_{13}$, i.e. a
> $p^e\parallel M$ with $13\mid p^e+1$. But for $\operatorname{ord}_{13}(p)=4$
> (i.e. $p\equiv5,8\bmod13$) Lemma V gives $D_{13}<0$ already at $e\equiv2\pmod4$ —
> in particular at $e=2$. So the $13$-adic condition imposes **no** constraint on
> the exponents, only the (weak) requirement that some prime of $M$ lies in a
> prescribed set of residue classes mod $13$.

This is worth recording: of $\{2,5,7,13\}$ only $2,5,7$ carry exponent
information, and $7$ is by far the strongest.

## 4. Lemma H — the $11$-lemma

> **Lemma H (PROVED, hand-checkable).** Suppose $11\mid M$ in (5). Then
> **(a)** $v_{11}(M)=2$; **(b)** every other prime of $M$ is $\ge127$;
> **(c)** $\omega(M)\ge4$.

*Proof.* **(a)** $\rho(11,3)=\frac{11^4-1}{10(11^3+1)}=\frac{14640}{13320}=\frac{122}{111}$
and $122\cdot91=11102>11100=100\cdot111$, so
$\rho(11,3)-\tfrac{100}{91}=\tfrac{2}{10101}>0$. Since $\rho(11,\cdot)$ is strictly
increasing and every factor of (5) is $\le\tau$, $e\ge3$ is impossible; $M$ is
powerful, so $e=2$.

**(b)** The residual is $\tau':=\dfrac{100/91}{133/122}=\dfrac{12200}{12103}$ with
$12103=91\cdot133=7^2\cdot13\cdot19$, $\gcd(12200,12103)=1$, and
$\tau'-1=\tfrac{97}{12103}$. Any other prime $q$ of $M$ satisfies
$\rho(q,2)\le\rho(q,f)\le\tau'$, i.e. $\frac{q}{q^2+1}\le\frac{97}{12103}$, i.e.
$$97q^2-12103q+97\ \ge\ 0 .$$
The quadratic has roots $\approx0.0080$ and $\approx124.77$; explicitly it equals
$-9203$ at $q=124$ and $+2847$ at $q=125$. Hence $q\ge125$, so $q\ge127$.

**(c)** $\omega(M)=1$ is C3. $\omega(M)=2$: then $\rho(q,f)=\tau'$ in lowest terms,
so by v2's (4) $q\mid 12200-12103=97$, i.e. $q=97<127$, contradicting (b). (This
is v2's C4, re-derived.) $\omega(M)=3$ is excluded by Theorem F below. $\square$

The margin $2/10101$ in (a) is worth flagging: $11^3$ misses being admissible by
one part in $5\cdot10^3$. It is the single tightest inequality in the whole
problem, and it is what makes the prime $11$ tractable at all.

**Companion bound (PROVED).** If $\omega(M)=k$ then the least prime $p_1$ of $M$
satisfies $P(p_1)^k>\tau$, since $\prod P(p_i)>\prod\rho(p_i,e_i)=\tau$ and
$P$ is decreasing. Concretely $91p^3>100(p-1)^3$ fails first at $p=37$
($91\cdot37^3-100\cdot36^3=-56177$), so
$$k=2\Rightarrow p_1\le19,\qquad k=3\Rightarrow p_1\le31,\qquad k=4\Rightarrow p_1\le41 .$$

## 5. Theorem F — $\omega(M)\le3$ is impossible

> **Theorem F (COMPUTATIONAL — exact rational arithmetic, no exponent bound, no
> prime bound, no floating point).** There is no powerful $M$ coprime to $6$ with
> $\prod_{p^e\parallel M}\rho(p,e)=100/91$ and $\omega(M)\le3$.

> **Corollary F1.** If $6\mid n$, $n$ is a powerful member of $A$ and
> $\omega(n)\le5$, then $n=108$.

*(F1 from F: by Lemma C, $n\ne108$ forces $n=36M$, so $\omega(n)=2+\omega(M)$.)*
This extends v2's Theorem E, which reached $\omega(n)\le4$.

### 5.1 Status and scope, stated exactly

Theorem F is decided by v2's **engine 2** (exact residual + exponent-free tail),
run on $\tau=100/91$ from the prime $11$ upward, with two changes:

* **(new)** the prime-index bounds are computed by exact `Fraction` comparisons
  instead of double-precision logarithms with a slack. Every comparison in the
  whole pipeline — `bounds`, `window_last`, `rho_sup`, `solve_set`, the last-prime
  scan — is now integer or exact-rational. *There is no floating point in PART 4.*
* the exponent split $E$ is bookkeeping only (v2 §6): exponents $2\le e\le E$ are
  handled with the exact residual, the tail $e\ge E+1$ by support enumeration
  finished with `solve_set`, which is complete and unconditional.

The run reports **0 gaps** at $\omega(M)=1,2,3$ — i.e. every branch was closed —
and no solutions. It is stable under $E\in\{3,4,6,8,10\}$ and sieve limits
$5\cdot10^4$ to $3\cdot10^6$; the widest last-prime window encountered was $8484$
integers. A **positive control** is included: the identical engine run on $\tau=2$
with $\omega=2$ returns exactly $108=2^2\cdot3^3$.

The only non-arithmetic ingredient is deterministic Miller–Rabin primality
(exact for $n<3.3\cdot10^{24}$; every integer tested here is $<10^5$).

### 5.2 Human-readable skeleton of the $\omega(M)=3$ case

Write $M=p^eq^fr^g$, $11\le p<q<r$.

1. $P(p)^3>\tau$ gives $p\le31$: seven candidates $11,13,17,19,23,29,31$.
2. $p=11$: Lemma H gives $e=2$, residual $\tau'=\tfrac{12200}{12103}$, and
   $127\le q$; and $P(q)^2>\tau'$ gives $q\le251$ (the largest prime with
   $P(q)^2>\tau'$ is exactly $251$). For each such $q$ one has $P(q)<\tau'$, so
   *all* exponents $f$ are admissible — but the residual left for $r$ then lies in
   the **exact interval** $\bigl(\tau'/P(q),\ \tau'/\rho(q,2)\bigr]$, whose lower
   end exceeds $1$, and v2's last-prime window turns that into an explicit finite
   range for $r$.
3. $p\ge13$: $P(p)<\tau$, so again all $e$ are admissible; the residual after
   $p^e$ lies in $\bigl(\tau/P(p),\ \tau/\rho(p,2)\bigr]$ and the same two-step
   argument applies. A worked instance, $p=13$, $q=53$:
   $\rho(13,3)\rho(53,2)=\tfrac{48671}{44117}>\tau$, so $e=2$ and the residual is
   exactly $\tau/\rho(13,2)=\tfrac{17000}{16653}$; then $P(53)=\tfrac{53}{52}$ is
   $<\tfrac{17000}{16653}$, so every $f$ is admissible, but the residual for $r$
   lies in $\bigl(\tfrac{68000}{67893},\ \tfrac{47770000}{47677539}\bigr]$, whence
   $516\le r\le635$ — a finite check.

It is this "exact-interval, then exact-residual" alternation that closes what
v2's engine 1 could not: v2 §7 (S1) reported that at $\omega(M)=3$ the *support*
enumeration first becomes infinite, leaving $2973$ supports and no verdict.
The infinity is an artefact of propagating an interval lower bound
$t_{\mathrm{lo}}\mapsto t_{\mathrm{lo}}/P(p)$ (which can fall below $1$); working
with the exact residual removes it at $\omega(M)=3$. **It does not remove it at
$\omega(M)=4$** (§7).

### 5.3 What Theorem F does *not* cover

Nothing about $\omega(M)\ge4$. In particular the target sub-problem T1 remains
open; Theorem F only pushes the first open case from $\omega(M)=3$ to
$\omega(M)=4$, i.e. from $\omega(n)=5$ to $\omega(n)=6$.

## 6. T2 — the Zsygmondy / factor-chain route of Obstacle 1

Throughout this section $n$ is a powerful member of $A$ with $2^a\parallel n$,
$a\ge2$, and $\mathcal A:=2^{a+1}$. (By Lemma C, $a\ge3$ forces $3\nmid n$, so this
section is about the branch complementary to §§2–5.)

### 6.1 The divisibility is real

> **Lemma Z1 (PROVED).** With $S=\prod_{p^e\parallel n,\ p\ \mathrm{odd}}\sigma(p^e)$
> and $U=\prod_{p^e\parallel n,\ p\ \mathrm{odd}}(p^e+1)$,
> $$\frac SU=\frac{2}{\rho(2,a)}=\frac{\mathcal A+2}{\mathcal A-1},\qquad
> \gcd(\mathcal A+2,\mathcal A-1)=\gcd(3,\mathcal A-1)=\begin{cases}3,&a\text{ odd},\\1,&a\text{ even}.\end{cases}$$
> Hence $\dfrac{\mathcal A-1}{\gcd(3,\mathcal A-1)}\ \Big|\ U$. In particular **every
> prime $\ell>3$ dividing $2^{a+1}-1$ divides $\prod_{p^e\parallel n,\,p\ \mathrm{odd}}(p^e+1)$**
> — v2's Obstacle-1 claim, now proved. (The unqualified statement
> $2^{a+1}-1\mid U$ holds when $a$ is even; for odd $a$ only $(2^{a+1}-1)/3\mid U$
> is guaranteed.)

*Proof.* $\rho(2,a)=\frac{2^{a+1}-1}{2^a+1}$, so
$2/\rho(2,a)=\frac{2(2^a+1)}{2^{a+1}-1}=\frac{\mathcal A+2}{\mathcal A-1}$; and
$\gcd(\mathcal A+2,\mathcal A-1)=\gcd(3,\mathcal A-1)$, with $3\mid2^{a+1}-1$ iff
$a+1$ is even. $\square$

### 6.2 The sharpening supplied by Lemma V

> **Lemma Z2 (PROVED).** For every prime $\ell>3$ with $\ell\mid2^{a+1}-1$,
> $$\sum_{p^e\parallel n,\ p\ \mathrm{odd}}D_\ell(p,e)\;=\;-\,v_\ell(2^{a+1}-1)\;\le\;-1 .$$
> Hence some odd $p^e\parallel n$ has $D_\ell(p,e)<0$, and then by Lemma V
> $\operatorname{ord}_\ell(p)=2s$ is even with $e\equiv s\pmod{2s}$; in particular
> $p^s\equiv-1\pmod\ell$, so $p^s\ge\ell-1$.

*Proof.* $v_\ell(S)-v_\ell(U)=v_\ell(\mathcal A+2)-v_\ell(\mathcal A-1)$, and
$\ell>3$ with $\ell\mid\mathcal A-1$ gives $\ell\nmid\mathcal A+2$ because
$\gcd(\mathcal A+2,\mathcal A-1)\mid3$. The rest is Lemma V. $\square$

> **Theorem Z3 (Bang 1886 / Zsygmondy 1892, applied).** For $a+1\ge7$ the number
> $2^{a+1}-1$ has a primitive prime divisor $\ell_0$ (i.e.
> $\operatorname{ord}_{\ell_0}(2)=a+1$); then $\ell_0\equiv1\pmod{a+1}$, so
> $\ell_0\ge a+2\ge8$, hence $\ell_0\ge11$ and Lemma Z2 applies to $\ell_0$.

(The exceptional exponents for $2^m-1$ are $m=1$ and $m=6$; verified in PART 5 for
$m\le40$ by explicit factorisation, together with $\ell_0\equiv1\bmod m$.)

### 6.3 A new elementary bound that dominates it

> **Lemma Z4 (PROVED, new).** Every odd prime $p\mid n$ satisfies
> $$p\;>\;\frac{2^{a+1}-4}{3},\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad 3p+4>2^{a+1}.$$

*Proof.* $\prod_{p\ \mathrm{odd}}\rho(p,e)=2/\rho(2,a)=1+\frac{3}{2^{a+1}-1}$. For
$x_i>0$, $\prod(1+x_i)\ge1+\sum x_i$, so
$\sum_{p\ \mathrm{odd}}(\rho(p,e)-1)\le\frac{3}{2^{a+1}-1}$. Since $n$ is powerful,
$e\ge2$, and $\rho(p,e)-1\ge\rho(p,2)-1=\frac{p}{p^2+1}>\frac1{p+1}$ (as $p>1$).
Hence $\sum 1/(p_i+1)<3/(2^{a+1}-1)$, and each single term obeys the same bound. $\square$

Lemma Z4 generalises the $\omega(n)=3$-specific estimate quoted in v2's Obstacle 1
($2^a<p(3p-1)/(2(p-1))$) to **every** $\omega(n)$ and **every** odd prime of $n$,
and it is proved by two lines of inequality rather than by the mod-$q$ relation (4).

### 6.4 Why the route cannot bound $a$ — the wall, quantified

> **Proposition Z5 (PROVED).** Let $\ell>3$, $\ell\mid2^{a+1}-1$, and let $p^e$ be
> the prime power supplied by Lemma Z2, with $\operatorname{ord}_\ell(p)=2s$.
> * If $s\ge2$, then Lemma Z2's conclusion $p^s\ge\ell-1$ is **weaker** than Lemma
>   Z4: indeed $\ell\le2^{a+1}-1$ gives $p>\frac{\ell-3}{3}$, and
>   $\bigl(\frac{\ell-3}{3}\bigr)^2\ge\ell-1$ as soon as $\ell\ge17$ (the criterion
>   is $\ell^2-15\ell+18\ge0$, which fails only for $\ell\in\{5,7,11,13\}$ — and
>   those $\ell$ are bounded, so they cannot bound $a$ either).
> * If $s=1$ (i.e. $p\equiv-1\bmod\ell$), Lemma Z2 gives $p\ge\ell-1$, which
>   improves Lemma Z4 by a factor of at most $3$.
>
> Hence the primitive-divisor / factor-chain conditions **cannot** bound
> $a=v_2(n)$: they are, up to a bounded factor, already implied by an elementary
> inequality that itself does not bound $a$.

The counting version is equally decisive. For $\omega(n)=3$ (say $n=2^ap^eq^f$),
Lemma Z4 together with $P(p)^2>\frac{\mathcal A+2}{\mathcal A-1}$ confines $p$ to
$$\Bigl(\tfrac{2^{a+1}-4}{3},\ \tfrac{2(2^{a+1}+2)}{3}\Bigr),$$
an interval of length $\asymp 2^{a+1}/3$ holding $\asymp\frac{2^{a+1}}{3(a+1)\log2}$
primes, whereas $2^{a+1}-1$ has $O(a)$ prime factors and each condition
$\ell\mid p^e+1$ is a congruence condition satisfied by a positive proportion of
them. PART 5 prints the table; e.g.

```
  a=16 :  window (43690, 87382)   ~3840 primes    l>3 dividing 2^17-1 : [131071]
  a=20 :  window (699050, 1398102) ~49400 primes  l>3 dividing 2^21-1 : [7, 127, 337]
  a=22 :  window (2796202, 5592406) ~179971 primes l>3 dividing 2^23-1 : [47, 178481]
```

So there is no counting obstruction, and none can be manufactured from these
conditions alone. **This is a negative result about the route named in v2's
Obstacle 1, and it is the main T2 finding: that route is correct but cannot
deliver a bound on $v_2(n)$.**

What *could* still work with Zsygmondy is the complementary direction — primitive
divisors of $p^{e+1}-1$ feeding a *factor chain*:

> **Lemma Z6 (PROVED, recorded for future use).** Let $p^e\parallel n$ with $p$
> odd and let $\ell$ be an **odd** prime with $\ell\mid\sigma(p^e)$. Then
> $$\ell\ \Big|\ \prod_{q^f\parallel n}(q^f+1)\;=\;(2^a+1)\prod_{q^f\parallel n,\ q\ \mathrm{odd}}(q^f+1).$$
> In particular if $\ell\nmid 2^a+1$ then some **odd** $q^f\parallel n$ satisfies
> $q^f\equiv-1\pmod\ell$. If moreover $\ell$ is a *primitive* prime divisor of
> $p^{e+1}-1$ (which exists for every $p$ and $e+1\ge2$ outside the Zsygmondy
> exceptions), then $\operatorname{ord}_\ell(p)=e+1$, hence $\ell\nmid p-1$, hence
> indeed $\ell\mid\sigma(p^e)$, and $\ell\equiv1\pmod{e+1}$, so $\ell\ge e+2$.

*Proof.* Take $v_\ell$ of $\prod_{q^f\parallel n}\sigma(q^f)=2\prod_{q^f\parallel n}(q^f+1)$;
the left side has $v_\ell\ge v_\ell(\sigma(p^e))\ge1$ and $v_\ell(2)=0$. $\square$

This is the standard odd-perfect-number factor chain. It is *finitely* branching
only when $\omega(n)$ is bounded, which is Obstacle 2 — see §7.

## 7. Named obstacles (updated)

**Obstacle 1 (updated — now understood).** v2 proposed bounding $v_2(n)$ via
"every prime $\ell>3$ of $2^{a+1}-1$ divides $\prod(p^e+1)$". §6 proves that claim
(Z1), sharpens it to an exact $\ell$-adic identity (Z2), and then **proves that it
cannot bound $a$** (Z5): it is dominated, up to a factor $3$, by the elementary
Lemma Z4, and the admissible window for the odd primes contains exponentially many
candidates versus $O(a)$ congruence conditions. *This obstacle should therefore be
retired as a proof strategy.* Bounding $v_2(n)$ needs a genuinely multiplicative
input, not a size or divisibility count.

**Obstacle 2 (moved by one step).** The target (5) is still open. Theorem F
closes $\omega(M)\le3$; the first open case is now
$$\omega(M)=4:\quad \rho(p_1,e_1)\rho(p_2,e_2)\rho(p_3,e_3)\rho(p_4,e_4)=\tfrac{100}{91},
\quad 11\le p_1\le41,\ \gcd(p_i,6)=1 .$$
By Lemma H, if $p_1=11$ then $e_1=2$ and $p_2,p_3,p_4\ge127$.

**Obstacle 3 (relocated).** The residual-hugs-$1$ phenomenon that v2 met at
$\omega(M)=3$ is removed there by the exact-residual engine, and reappears at
$\omega(M)=4$.

*Exploratory only* — not part of the shipped script, over the compute budget, and
run with v2's original floating-point index bounds rather than v3's exact ones:
engine 2 on $\tau=100/91$ with $\omega(M)=4$ finds **no solutions** but leaves
$31$ branches unclosed at $E=6$ ($131$ s) and $61$ at $E=12$ ($163$ s). Raising
$E$ makes it *worse*, because each additional exactly-handled exponent spawns its
own interval tail. The unclosed branches are all of the form "a prefix $p_1^{e_1}$,
then a prime $p_2$ whose exponent tail is only known by an interval", e.g.

```
('support', ((11, 2),), 127)   ('support', ((11, 2),), 131)   ('support', ((11, 2),), 179)
('support', ((11, 2),), 191)   ('support', ((11, 2),), 197)   ('support', ((11, 2),), 227)
('support', ((13, 3),),  71)   ('support', ((13, 3),),  73)   ('support', ((13, 3),), 109)
('support', ((13, 4),),  71)   ...
```

i.e. a two-prime prefix leaving a residual between $1+8\cdot10^{-5}$ and
$1+6\cdot10^{-3}$ for the last two primes. **This is recorded as evidence, not as
a theorem**, and it does not establish anything about $\omega(M)=4$.

**Obstacle 4 (new — the exact shape of the $\omega(M)=4$ gap).**
First a positive result, which explains why $\omega(M)\le3$ closed at all:

> **Lemma W (PROVED).** Let $t=1+\varepsilon$ be an **exact** rational with
> $\varepsilon>0$, and consider $\rho(q,f)\rho(r,g)=t$ in unknown primes $q<r$ and
> unknown exponents $f,g\ge2$. Then the solution set is finite and computable,
> provided $P(q)\ne t$:
> * if $q<1+1/\varepsilon$ then $P(q)>t$, so $\rho(q,f)\le t$ holds for only
>   finitely many $f$; each $f$ gives an exact residual and hence at most two
>   candidates for $r$ (v2 §6);
> * if $q>1+1/\varepsilon$ then $f$ is unbounded, but the residual for $r$ lies in
>   $\bigl(t/P(q),\,t/\rho(q,2)\bigr]$ whose lower end is $>1$, so
>   $r<1+\bigl(t/P(q)-1\bigr)^{-1}$.
>
> Only $q=1+1/\varepsilon$ (i.e. $P(q)=t$ exactly) escapes both horns; the engine
> reports that as a gap, and it never occurred for $\tau=100/91$, $\omega(M)\le3$.

The obstruction at $\omega(M)=4$ is therefore **not** the two-prime problem but
the loss of exactness one level up. For a prime $p$ with $P(p)\le\tau_{\text{res}}$
the exponent $e$ is unbounded, so after $p$ the residual is known only to lie in an
*interval* $\bigl(t/P(p),\,t/\rho(p,E+1)\bigr]$; and with interval data the
dichotomy of Lemma W collapses precisely for the one or two primes $q$ with
$q\approx 1+1/\varepsilon'$, $\varepsilon'=t/P(p)-1$: there neither $f$ nor $r$ can
be bounded. All $31$ ($E=6$) resp. $61$ ($E=12$) unclosed branches are of exactly
this shape; e.g. for the branch $\bigl(11^2,127^{e}\bigr)$ one has
$t=\tfrac{12200}{12103}$, $t/P(127)=\tfrac{219600}{219583}$, so
$\varepsilon'=\tfrac{17}{219583}$ and $1+1/\varepsilon'=\tfrac{219600}{17}=12917.6\ldots$:
the bad primes sit at $q\approx12918$.

**The exact statement that is missing** is therefore:

> Bound $r$ (or $f$) when $\rho(p,e)\rho(q,f)\rho(r,g)$ is required to lie in a
> prescribed half-open interval $(t_{\mathrm{lo}},t_{\mathrm{up}}]$ with
> $t_{\mathrm{lo}}>1$, rather than to equal a prescribed exact rational.

**Why the available tools cannot supply it.** The two necessary conditions of
v2 §6 differ, to first order, by $\sum1/p^2$ (v2 Obstacle 2), so no inequality of
that shape separates them. The exact conditions available — the mod-$p_j$ relation
(4) and the $\ell$-adic identities V1/V2 of §2 — are *congruence* conditions on
$(p,e,q,f,r,g)$, and congruence conditions are satisfied by a positive proportion
of the admissible window (this is the same phenomenon §6.4 quantifies for
Obstacle 1). What is missing is an input that couples the *sizes* of the primes to
the *arithmetic* of the exact cancellation — e.g. a bound on $\omega(M)$, or a
non-trivial lower bound for
$$\bigl|\,91\textstyle\prod_{p^e\parallel M}\sigma(p^e)\;-\;100\prod_{p^e\parallel M}(p^e+1)\,\bigr|$$
whenever it is non-zero. That is a linear-forms-in-logarithms / $abc$-flavoured
statement, and §6 shows that Zsygmondy alone does not provide it.
